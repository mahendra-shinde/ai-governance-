# Module 7: Recap and Transition to Governance

## Learning Objectives
- Recap the Day 1 harm framework as the foundation for Day 2's regulatory and governance content.
- Explain the shift from voluntary ethics to mandatory compliance, and what that shift changes in practice.

---

## 7.1 Review of the Day 1 Harm Framework

Day 1 established the diagnostic foundation this course relies on for everything that follows:

- **Module 1** framed Responsible AI as an operational and legal concern, with accountability already distributed across the organisation.
- **Module 2** gave the vocabulary — AI/ML/DL, foundation/generative/agentic models, predictive vs. generative failure modes, and the Provider/Deployer/Distributor/Affected Person role taxonomy.
- **Module 3** grounded the discussion in five concrete failure patterns drawn from real-world categories of incident.
- **Module 4** decomposed harm into four root sources: data, model, deployment context, and human interaction.
- **Module 5** showed that Responsible AI principles trade off against one another, and that fairness itself is not a single definable target but a business decision requiring explicit ownership.
- **Module 6** consolidated all of this against participants' own real use cases.

**The thread connecting all of it:** harm is diagnosable, has identifiable root causes, and requires conscious, owned decisions to mitigate — it is not an unavoidable side effect of "using AI" that organisations must simply accept.

Day 2 now turns from *diagnosing* harm to *governing* it: what does the law require, what frameworks exist to operationalise good practice, and how do you build a function that keeps these controls running continuously rather than as a one-time exercise?

## 7.2 From Voluntary Ethics to Mandatory Compliance

For much of the 2010s, "AI ethics" existed mainly as voluntary corporate principles — high-level statements about fairness, transparency, and human-centricity, published without binding enforcement mechanisms, audit requirements, or penalties.

That era is ending. Three shifts mark the transition:

1. **From principles to binding law.** The EU AI Act (Module 8) is the first comprehensive, binding, cross-sectoral AI law with real penalties. It converts previously voluntary principles — risk management, transparency, human oversight — into enforceable legal obligations with specific deadlines.
2. **From self-assessment to external assurance.** Standards like ISO/IEC 42001 (Module 10) introduce certifiable management systems, meaning organisations can be audited by accredited third parties against a defined standard, not just asked to self-report.
3. **From a single jurisdiction to a global patchwork.** Guidelines such as India's MeitY AI Governance Guidelines (Module 9), alongside sectoral regulators and an expanding set of national frameworks, mean organisations operating across borders must now map and reconcile multiple regulatory regimes rather than rely on a single ethics statement.

**What this changes in practice:**

| | Voluntary Ethics Era | Mandatory Compliance Era |
|---|---|---|
| **Who decides the standard** | The organisation itself | Regulators, standards bodies, courts |
| **Evidence required** | A published statement of principles | Documented risk assessments, technical files, audit trails |
| **Consequence of failure** | Reputational criticism | Fines, injunctions, litigation, decertification |
| **Review cadence** | Ad hoc | Defined by law/standard (e.g., continuous monitoring, periodic audit) |
| **Who is accountable** | Diffuse / unclear | Named roles under statute (Provider, Deployer) and under management standards (top management, AI governance function) |

This is the lens through which Modules 8–13 should be read: not as abstract legal theory, but as the operational machinery that has replaced "good intentions" as the basis for managing AI risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Day 1 built a diagnostic toolkit: terminology, failure patterns, root causes of harm, and principle trade-offs.
- Day 2 builds the governance toolkit: binding regulation, certifiable standards, and the organisational function needed to operate them continuously.
- The shift from voluntary ethics to mandatory compliance changes who sets the standard, what evidence is required, what the consequences of failure are, and who is explicitly accountable.
