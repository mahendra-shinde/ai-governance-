# Module 1: Introduction and Course Framing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, participants will be able to:
- Explain why Responsible AI is now an operational and legal concern, not only an ethical one.
- Identify who inside an organisation already carries accountability for AI outcomes.
- Describe the scope and boundaries of this workshop.

---

## 1.1 Why Responsible AI Has Moved from an Ethical Concern to an Operational One

For much of the last decade, "AI ethics" was treated as a voluntary, reputational topic — something handled by a corporate responsibility team, a set of principles published on a website, or a one-off training session. That framing no longer matches reality.

Three forces have converted Responsible AI from a soft commitment into a hard operational requirement:

1. **Binding regulation.** Laws such as the EU AI Act impose enforceable obligations, with fines that can reach into the tens of millions of euros or a percentage of global turnover. Guidelines such as India's MeitY AI Governance Guidelines, while not yet carrying direct penalties, signal the direction of statutory travel and already shape procurement and sectoral regulatory expectations.
2. **Litigation and enforcement exposure.** Hiring tools, credit models, and welfare-eligibility systems have already been the subject of lawsuits and regulatory action. Liability does not wait for a dedicated "AI law" — existing discrimination, consumer protection, and data protection law already apply to algorithmic decisions.
3. **Operational fragility at scale.** When a human caseworker makes an error, the damage is usually contained to one case. When an automated system makes the same category of error, it can apply that error to thousands or millions of people simultaneously, before anyone notices. Scale turns a quality defect into an incident.

The practical consequence is that Responsible AI now has to be engineered into the same governance, risk, and control structures that already manage financial risk, cybersecurity risk, and data protection risk — with named owners, documented controls, audit trails, and board-level reporting.

## 1.2 Who Is Already Accountable for AI Inside an Organisation

A common misconception is that AI accountability sits only with the data science or engineering team that built the model. In practice, accountability is distributed, and regulation increasingly makes this explicit:

- **The Board and senior management** — accountable for setting risk appetite, approving the AI governance framework, and ensuring adequate resourcing. Under frameworks like ISO/IEC 42001 and the EU AI Act, "top management" has named obligations.
- **Business/product owners** — accountable for the use case, its purpose, and the decisions made using the system's output, even if they did not build the model.
- **Data science and engineering teams** — accountable for technical design choices: data selection, model architecture, evaluation metrics.
- **Procurement and vendor management** — accountable for third-party and vendor AI risk, since most organisations deploy AI built by someone else.
- **Legal, risk, and compliance functions** — accountable for regulatory mapping, contractual protections, and incident response.
- **Internal audit** — accountable for independent assurance that controls are operating as designed.

The workshop will repeatedly return to one idea: **AI accountability already exists in your organisation today, distributed across these roles — the job of AI governance is to make that accountability explicit, documented, and connected, not to invent it from nothing.**

## 1.3 Scope of the Workshop

### What this workshop covers
- A practical understanding of how AI systems fail in the real world (Module 3, Module 4).
- The core Responsible AI principles and why they involve genuine trade-offs, not just checklists (Module 5).
- The current regulatory landscape: the EU AI Act, India's AI governance guidelines, and the global patchwork (Modules 8–9).
- Recognised frameworks and standards — NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 23894, OECD AI Principles (Module 10).
- The specific risks introduced by generative and agentic AI (Module 11).
- How to design and operate an AI governance function inside an organisation (Module 12).

### What this workshop deliberately does not cover
- It is **not** a machine learning engineering course — model training, architecture design, and MLOps tooling are out of scope.
- It is **not** legal advice. Regulatory content is provided for operational awareness; organisations must obtain jurisdiction-specific legal review for compliance decisions.
- It does **not** provide vendor or tool recommendations — frameworks are covered at the level of principles and structure, not specific commercial products.
- It does **not** assume participants will become AI Act or legal experts; the goal is working fluency sufficient to ask the right questions and recognise red flags.

---

## Key Takeaways
- Responsible AI failures are now regulatory, legal, and operational failures — not just reputational ones.
- Accountability for AI already exists across the organisation; governance makes it explicit and traceable.
- This course builds practical governance literacy, not legal expertise or ML engineering skill.

## Discussion Prompt
Think of one AI-enabled tool or decision system already in use in your function (even a simple scoring spreadsheet or chatbot). Who would be accountable today if it produced a harmful or incorrect outcome? Is that clear, or does it need to be clarified?
